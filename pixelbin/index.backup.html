How should people
interact with AI?

What We Believed

The Feature
List

We believed the question was "which features should the editor have." Background removal. Upscaling. Watermark removal. Generation. The feature list felt like the product decision.

The feature list was identical across every competitor. The differentiation was not what the tool could do. It was how the user communicated with it.

We had to stop designing features and start choosing a worldview.

What Challenged That Belief

Three
Paradigms

Three fundamentally different interaction philosophies existed. Each assumed something different about the user. Each excluded someone. The design question was not "which paradigm is best" but "which assumption are you willing to make about your users — and which users are you willing to lose."

01

Canvas

Rejected

The Paradigm

The spatial approach — users place, arrange, and edit assets on an infinite canvas. We studied Flora Fauna, Remake, and Google Mixboard. Then we prototyped 170 frames of this paradigm ourselves.

Magic Canvas POC → Spatial workspace prototype

What the Research Revealed

Highly visual and tangible. Ideal for multi-asset campaigns — users could drag concepts around, compare variants side-by-side, and keep all assets in a single project. A board equals a campaign. Good mental model for e-commerce workflows.

But overwhelming for non-creatives. If someone just wants to remove a background and export in three sizes, the canvas feels like overkill. Performance-heavy on lower-end machines. And it conflicts with the wizard-style flows that SMB users prefer.

Verdict

Strong for professional creators who work with multiple assets spatially. Wrong for the majority of Pixelbin's users — people who want quick, guided results, not an infinite workspace.

02

Chat

Rejected

The Paradigm

The conversational approach — describe what you want in natural language and the AI creates it. We studied Google AI Studio, Wix AI Chat, Lumalabs, and Lovart.

Competitors studied → Chat-based editors

What the Research Revealed

Extremely low barrier to start. Minimal UI complexity — mostly input, result, refine. Mobile-friendly and easy to wrap into templated workflows. Fast experimentation for users who are good at prompting.

But prompt quality becomes a gatekeeper. Small business users struggle to describe what they want — "make it more premium but not too premium" is vague. Harder to control layout and micro details through text alone. Editing feels abstract. And learnability is fragile — changes in model behavior feel like the tool changed.

Verdict

Accessible for advanced users who think in language. Excluding for everyone who thinks in images. If you can't describe what you want, you can't get it.

03

Prompt
+ Tool

Selected

The Paradigm

The hybrid — a prompt bar for broad strokes, precision tools for fine control. We studied Krea, Runway ML, Playground, Pixelcut, and OpenArt. This was the paradigm we ultimately shipped.

PB 3.0 → Image Editor → Shipped production interface

What the Research Revealed

Best of both worlds — prompt for big moves, tools for precision. Gentle learning curve for users coming from existing image editors. Clear mapping between regions and prompts: select an area, describe the change, only that region updates. Supports both quick tasks and deep editing. Great fit for template-based use cases like AI Photoshoots and e-commerce banners.

But the warning was clear: UI complexity risk. Canvas plus toolbar plus layers plus prompt box plus model selector — it can get crowded. Users might not know when to use AI versus manual controls. Performance and state management complexity across undo, redo, and large images. This warning would prove prophetic — 103 usability issues later.

Verdict

The strongest candidate. Selected for production. But only because the team was willing to fight the complexity warning through 103 rounds of QA and iteration.

Three paradigms.
Four user types.
One decision.

What We Discovered

Four User
Types

We mapped four user types by their relationship to prompting — not by demographics, not by industry. By how they relate to the act of telling an AI what to do. The taxonomy emerged from 4 user interviews, competitor research across 9 products, and analysis of Pixelbin's existing usage patterns — not from a formal survey, but from pattern recognition across qualitative data.

  1. 01Users who know what they want and how to prompt for it
  2. 02Users who know how to prompt but don't have a use case
  3. 03Users who don't know how to prompt
  4. 04Users who don't know what AI can make

PB Research 3.0 → User type taxonomy

No single paradigm serves all four. The question was not "which paradigm is best." It was "which assumption are you willing to make about your users."

The Decision

Two
Paradigms

Canvas was rejected for the consumer product. Chat was rejected. The Prompt + Tool hybrid was selected for production.

But the Canvas paradigm didn't die. It became the power-user vision. Two paradigms serving two audiences, informed by a deliberate study of three. And then we chose a different one for production.

The decision was not to abandon the Canvas paradigm. It was to assign each to the audience it serves best.

What if the user doesn't
know what to ask?

What We Believed

The Prompt
Box

We believed the prompt box was the product. Type what you want. Get what you described. The AI is as good as the instruction it receives.

We watched four user types approach that prompt box. Type 1 — people who know what they want and how to ask — thrived. They were maybe 15% of the user base. Everyone else froze.

Our most powerful tool had a blank input box as its front door. We were asking people to learn a new language before they could use the product.

What We Discovered

Propose,
Don't Wait

The solution wasn't better prompts. It was removing the need to prompt.

We designed Pixie — an AI that proposes instead of waiting. When a user uploads an image, Pixie analyzes it and suggests specific actions. The user never writes a prompt. They approve a proposal.

We designed a backbone flow where Pixie interprets vague input, proposes a complete plan, asks for context once, creates the assets, then explains WHY it made each design decision — like an actual designer presenting their rationale.

The Evidence

Pixie's
States

Pixie wasn't a feature. It was a complete state system. Eight toast states for every moment the AI might need to communicate. Proactive suggestions that turn uploads into conversations.

On mobile, we put suggestion pills on the Home screen. Pre-written prompts for people who can't write their own. The user who knows what to type still types. The user who doesn't is already being helped.

What happens when
the AI gets it wrong?

What We Believed

Edge
Cases

We believed that if the AI model was good enough, failure states were edge cases. Design the happy path. Handle errors with a toast notification. Move on.

We shipped the Prompt+Tool editor. The paradigm research had warned us: "UI complexity risk." We noted the warning. We designed carefully. And the warning came true anyway.

What We Discovered

103
Issues

103 usability issues were documented directly in the design file. Not a backlog. Sticky notes on the Figma canvas, organized by tool.

"Entire screen is getting covered by the toolbar." "Brush tool is not even usable." "Camera angle auto-snaps to 45 degrees." These weren't AI failures. They were interaction failures that made the user think the AI was broken.

Separately, the onboarding flow had 10 documented UX failures. "Popup looks like error warning." "CTA says purchase without showing tools." "2 popups one over another — credits not explained."

The System

Designing
Failure

We built a taxonomy of failure. Interface failure: the tool itself is confusing. AI failure: the model generates something wrong. Resource failure: the user runs out of credits.

Every tool got paired success/failure messages — 12 variants in the Messages component. The credit bar got four emotional tiers, from "Plenty of pixel power left" to "It's time to refuel your creative powers." The model slow response got an explicit toast instead of an ambiguous spinner.

How do you scale
creativity?

What We Believed

More
Volume

We believed that scaling meant "the same interface, but it handles more images." Upload 50 instead of one. Apply the same edit. Progress bar. Done.

When one image fails, the user retries. When image 23 of 50 fails, the user doesn't know if they should restart the batch, skip that image, or change the settings. The single-image mental model breaks entirely.

What We Discovered

Decision
Reduction

Scale is not about handling more volume. It is about reducing decisions. The template system transforms "configure this 50 times" into "configure once, apply forever."

Then we faced a different scale problem. Pixelbin wasn't one product anymore. It was nine. The Foundation→Element→Assembly architecture transforms "design 9 products" into "design atoms, compose everything."

The Merge

Two Tools,
One Interface

When image and video generation merged into one interface, the cost was visible. The generation panel, model selector, history timeline, and empty states all had to change — separately for each generator. Then came the fixes: 25 for the image panel, 24 for video, 15 for image history, 14 for video history. Merging two AI tools was not a redesign. It was 78 production fixes.

The Process

How We
Handed Off

The 29 annotated tooltips were the handoff language we developed with engineering. Each tooltip specifies the interaction, the state change, and the edge case. Engineers reviewed annotations before sprint planning — disagreements were resolved in the Figma file, not in Slack. The annotation became the contract.

This process emerged from necessity. Early handoffs used separate spec documents that drifted from the designs within days. Moving specs into the Figma file as tooltip annotations meant the spec and the design were always in sync. When a design changed, the annotation changed with it. One source of truth.

Design Principle

Scale should eliminate decisions,
not multiply them.

What happens when you
redesign a product four times?

What We Believed

V1 Was
The Product

We believed V1 was the product. A traditional image editor. Upload, select a tool, edit, export. The bottom navigation had two tabs: Home and Gallery. It was clean. It worked.

AI changed what the product could do faster than the interface could communicate. V1 was an editor. Then batch processing arrived. Then AR. Then generative AI. Each required not just new features but a new architecture.

What We Discovered

Kill To
Evolve

V1: "This app edits images." V2: "This app edits images at scale." V3: "This app is a creative ecosystem." V4: "This app is an AI."

The V4 redesign required killing the V1 mental model. The Home screen became a prompt bar with suggestion pills. The bottom navigation added "Explore" — making discovery equal to creation.

The Explorations page has sections labeled "JUNK" and "Dump." The V1 file is marked with an X. These aren't failures. They're fossils. The product couldn't become V4 without killing V1.

Design Principle

A redesign that doesn't kill anything
isn't a redesign. It's decoration.

How do you explain three years
in sixty seconds?

What We Believed

Describe
Everything

We believed the homepage should describe the product accurately. List the features. Show the tools. Explain the technology. Be comprehensive.

Draft 70 was a simple headline and a call-to-action. Accurate. Generic. It could have been any AI tool's homepage. 35 iterations later, we were still searching.

What We Discovered

Six
Words

"AI Photo Editor" was accurate. It was also forgettable. The positioning pivot changed six words: "The #1 AI Media Generator." From tool to platform. From photo to media. From editor to generator.

The product didn't change between draft 70 and draft 104. Only the understanding of what it was.

The final homepage puts the prompt bar in the hero. Visitors can try the product before reading about it. The interface became the marketing.

The Impact

132% Growth
in 30 Days

Landing page sessions grew from 4,556 to 10,567 daily unique sessions in a single 30-day period — a 132% increase. The repositioning from "AI Photo Editor" to "The #1 AI Media Generator" didn't just change the words. It changed the numbers.

96% of revenue is product-led. The design isn't just usable. It converts.
