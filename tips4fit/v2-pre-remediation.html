01 / The Question

Many fitness journeys span several specialist tools. Training in one, nutrition in another, activity and recovery somewhere else. The information rarely sits in one shared view.

What people currently use

MyFitnessPalNike Training ClubStravaApple HealthFitbitPelotonGymsharkMyProteinGoogle MapsYouTube FitnessHeadspaceSleep Cycle

The Tension

The problem is not
the individual apps.

Nike Training Club is good for workouts. MyFitnessPal handles macros. Strava tracks runs. Each works fine alone. The problem is they rarely share context across categories. Your training plan has limited awareness of what you ate. Your nutrition tracker does not know you skipped the gym. Your supplement purchases have little to do with your actual routine.

Tips4Fit explores what happens when training, nutrition, shopping, expert access, and progress tracking sit behind one profile. The dashboard pulls everything together. The shop reflects your goals. The trainer can see what you have been eating.

This is concept work. 24 screens across six modules, testing whether fitness benefits more from integration than from specialisation.

Evidence Boundaries

What was observable.
What remained a hypothesis.

What I knew

  • Fitness behaviour is already distributed across specialist products.
  • Training, nutrition and activity information often lives in separate experiences.
  • Existing products demonstrate demand for individual fitness activities.
  • Those disconnected activities create an opportunity to test whether shared context adds value.

What I assumed

  • People would want one product to connect or replace specialist tools.
  • A unified dashboard would help people understand their progress.
  • People would trust recommendations informed by their goals.
  • Users would want training, expert access and commerce in one experience.
  • The value of connection would outweigh the specialist depth of existing products.

The concept explores these assumptions. It does not claim to have validated them.

Strategic Focus

Start with
the Tracker.

All three profiles experience friction from fragmented fitness tools. But for the first test, I would focus on the Tracker: someone already recording training, nutrition and activity, but manually comparing information across separate services.

Core user job

"Help me understand how my training, nutrition and recovery affect one another without manually reconciling several apps."

Why this profile first

  • 01The fragmentation problem is already present. The Tracker already uses three or four apps. The fragmented workflow already exists, making the cost of manual comparison a focused hypothesis to test.
  • 02The user already values tracking. Adoption does not require teaching a new behaviour. It requires replacing manual cross-referencing with an integrated view.
  • 03The integration hypothesis is directly testable. Show this user a unified dashboard and measure whether they find connections they could not see before.
  • 04The unified view is comparable to their current workflow. The value proposition can be measured against what they already do, not against a behaviour they might adopt.

This is strategic prioritisation for a proposed first test. It is not a research-backed segment decision.

Product Principles

Four rules
for the
concept.

  • 01Connect before expanding. Initial value comes from connecting information, not accumulating unrelated features. The dashboard pulls training, nutrition and activity into one view before adding features like live classes or social.
  • 02Ask progressively. Collect only what the current task requires. Onboarding asks for goal, experience level and training type. Body measurements, nutrition history and wearable connections come later, when their value is clear.
  • 03Explain recommendations. Users should understand why a workout, product or expert appears. The shop surfaces protein for strength goals; the training module filters by experience level. The reason should be visible.
  • 04Keep trusted actions familiar. Payments, bookings, account controls and privacy settings follow established interaction patterns. The checkout works like any e-commerce checkout. The booking flow works like any calendar-based booking.

These principles guided the concept. They have not been tested with users.

04 / How It Works

Six modules.
One profile.
Everything connected.

The Spine

One profile
feeds every
module.

The user profile is not a settings page. It is the data layer that connects every module in the app. When you set a goal during onboarding, the dashboard reorganises, the training module filters programmes, and the shop surfaces relevant products. When you log a meal, the dashboard updates. When you complete a workout, your progress feeds back into the profile.

Each module reads from and writes to the same context. That shared state is what separates Tips4Fit from six separate apps stitched together with deep links.

  • 01Onboarding writes goals, body metrics, and preferences to the profile. Every subsequent module inherits this context.
  • 02Dashboard reads training, nutrition, and activity data. Displays them in a single view calibrated to the user's goals.
  • 03Training filters challenges, programmes, and classes based on the goal set during onboarding. Completed sessions write back to the dashboard.
  • 04Explore uses location and training preferences to surface relevant gyms, trainers, and specialists nearby.
  • 05Shop reads the user's active training programme and nutritional gaps to surface contextual product recommendations.
  • 06Profile aggregates everything: goals, progress, purchase history, bookings, and notification preferences. The spine of the system.

Why This Matters

A fitness app that opens to a generic dashboard loses the user immediately. Tips4Fit asks what matters first: strength, weight loss, flexibility, or endurance. Those choices shape the dashboard layout and the training recommendations that follow. Four sign-in options keep the entry quick. Personalisation happens before content, not in a settings page buried three taps deep.

Sign up → Verify → Personalise

01Login
02Sign up
03Verify OTP
04Set goals
Design decision Personalisation before content. Goal selection shapes the dashboard, training recommendations, and shop suggestions from the first session.

Deep Dive

Ask less now.
Earn more
later.

Personalisation improves the first experience. It also increases the chance someone leaves before finishing onboarding. Every additional question is a decision point where a new user can decide the app is not worth the effort. The goal is to collect the minimum information needed to make the dashboard and training module useful on day one, and defer everything else until the user sees why it matters.

Three questions are essential at signup: primary fitness goal, current experience level and preferred training type. These shape the dashboard layout, filter training programmes by difficulty, and select the first recommended workout. Without them, the product falls back to a generic view that looks the same for every user.

Everything else can wait. Detailed body measurements are useful for progress tracking but not required to show a first dashboard. Nutrition history builds naturally through meal logging. Wearable connections involve device permissions that add friction during signup. Location is only relevant when the user opens the gym finder for the first time. Professional-access permissions should be prompted when booking a session, not speculatively during onboarding.

Optional questions appear with a short explanation of what they unlock. A user who skips body measurements still sees training and nutrition data on the dashboard. Missing widgets do not appear as empty placeholders; the layout adjusts to show what it has. Users can return to any deferred question from their profile at any time.

Whether this balance is right would need to be tested. The risk of asking too little is a bland first experience. The risk of asking too much is abandonment before the dashboard loads.

unified dashboard

The Design Decision

Most fitness apps show one thing well: steps, or calories, or workouts completed. Tips4Fit proposes a dashboard that combines training activity, nutrition intake, and daily metrics in the same view. Date selection lets you scroll through history. The calorie meter plots macros against targets. Activity cards change based on your goals rather than following a fixed layout.

Home → Nutrition → Activity → Progress

01Daily overview
02Meal tracking
03Activity summary
04Progress metrics
Design decision One dashboard, three data streams. Training, nutrition, and activity metrics appear together because fitness outcomes depend on all three.

Deep Dive

One screen.
Three data
streams.

The dashboard answers one question: how is my fitness progressing across training, nutrition and activity? Everything on the screen exists to serve that question. If a card or metric does not contribute, it does not belong on the home view.

The hierarchy places training activity first because it is the most actionable. A user opens the app to decide what to do today, and recent sessions or a scheduled workout answer that immediately. Nutrition sits second because it is the most frequently logged data stream. Activity metrics (steps, heart rate, sleep) appear third because they are mostly passive and collected automatically through device integrations.

The layout adapts to the user's primary goal. A strength-focused dashboard emphasises sets, reps and weight progression. A weight-loss dashboard leads with calorie balance and deficit tracking. An endurance dashboard foregrounds distance, pace and session duration trends. The underlying data is the same; the presentation hierarchy changes.

Recommendations on the dashboard explain their source. A suggested workout card would show why it appeared: "Suggested because your goal is strength and you completed 3 sessions this week." A shop recommendation would disclose its trigger: "Recommended for strength training." The user should never wonder why something is on their screen.

When information is missing, the dashboard shows what it has rather than displaying empty widgets. A user who has not logged meals sees training and activity data. A user without a wearable sees training and nutrition. Blank cards with "no data" labels waste space and erode confidence. Instead, clear prompts invite the user to add the missing data stream when they are ready.

Trust and Privacy

Fitness data is personal. Commerce recommendations carry commercial interest. The product must be honest about both.

Responsible Design

What this
product must
get right.

Fitness guidance versus medical advice. The product offers wellness suggestions, not clinical diagnoses. Any feature that flags nutritional deficiencies or health risks would need clinical validation before display. Tips4Fit is a fitness tool, not a medical device.

Expert verification. Trainers, nutritionists and doctors must have verifiable credentials before appearing in the directory. The concept proposes a verification badge, but the actual validation process would need specialist legal review before any professional could be listed.

Consent before sharing. No trainer or doctor sees user information until the user explicitly grants access for a specific session or relationship. Blanket permissions do not exist. Each professional relationship requires its own consent.

Commercial transparency. When a recommendation has a commercial relationship — sponsored product, affiliate link, promoted listing — the product must disclose it. "Suggested for you" and "Sponsored" are different categories and must be labelled differently.

Explainable recommendations. Users should see why a product or service was recommended and which inputs influenced the suggestion. A protein supplement recommendation should trace back to the user's strength training goal, not appear without context.

User correction. Users can edit the information that shapes their recommendations. Changing a goal should immediately update what appears in the dashboard, shop and training modules. Stale recommendations erode trust.

User Control

View

Data

View the information used to generate recommendations, including goals, training history and nutrition entries. Understand what the product knows and how it uses that information.

Change

Edit or

Update preferences, change goals, revoke professional access or hide specific recommendations at any time. Controls must be findable, not buried in a settings submenu.

05 / Design Language

Dark canvas. Bright data. The interface stays quiet so the content can be loud.

Visual System

Why dark,
why minimal.

Most fitness and health apps default to white backgrounds with clinical styling. I chose dark surfaces for three reasons: to create contrast for data visualisation (charts and progress indicators against a dark canvas), to reduce visual competition (the UI recedes so content comes forward), and to differentiate from the clinical white look most health apps default to.

The colour system is deliberately restrained. The dark background does the work. Accent colour (a coral-orange, #FF5E39) marks primary actions and active states. Data visualisations use the accent alongside muted greens and blues to separate macro categories without competing for attention. Typography stays in one family throughout: clean sans-serif at varying weights for hierarchy, optimised for readability on small screens during workouts.

Colour

Dark
Canvas

Near-black backgrounds (#1A1A1A range) with white text for primary content. The coral accent (#FF5E39) marks CTAs, active tabs, and data highlights. Muted tones for secondary information. The interface does not compete with the data it displays.

Typography

One
Family

Single sans-serif typeface across the entire app. Weight variation (regular, medium, bold) creates hierarchy without introducing visual noise. Numbers use tabular figures for alignment in dashboards and progress indicators. No decorative type.

Components

Cards,
Not Pages

Content is organised in cards with consistent corner radii and spacing. Training programmes, nutrition entries, shop products, and expert profiles all follow the same card pattern. Familiar structure across six different modules reduces the learning curve.

Brand Application

Identity
through
interaction.

A visual identity that only appears in marketing materials and splash screens is decoration. In Tips4Fit, the brand is expressed through how the product behaves: which elements demand attention, which recede, how progress feels, and whether the experience remains consistent across six different modules. The identity lives in the interaction patterns, not just the colour palette.

  • 01Energy. The coral accent (#FF5E39) is reserved for priority actions and progress completion. It appears on CTAs, active navigation states and achievement indicators. Used sparingly so it retains signal value.
  • 02Focus. Dark surfaces reduce competing chrome around workout and data content. During a training session or when reviewing nutrition data, the interface recedes. The content is the interface.
  • 03Consistency. Shared card structure, spacing and typography across training, dashboard, shop and profile. A user who understands the dashboard layout can navigate the shop without relearning the interaction model.
  • 04Momentum. Progress bars, completion states and streak indicators provide visible forward movement. The dashboard shows where you are against your goal, not just what you did today.

A button that passes WCAG but cannot be tapped with a sweaty thumb during a deadlift set has not solved the problem.

Judgment

What to build first, what could break, what to measure.

Scope

V1: Dashboard, training programmes, nutrition tracking, shop, gym finder. Core loop: train, track, buy, repeat.

Deferred: Live video classes (streaming infrastructure), wearable integration (device fragmentation), social features (community moderation). Each adds complexity without validating the core hypothesis.

Risks

Specialist app loyalty. Nike Training Club has brand trust that a new entrant cannot replicate overnight. Content production. Workout programmes need certified trainers, which means ongoing cost. Shop margins. Competing with Amazon and Gymshark on price is not viable. Expert verification. Trainer and doctor credentials require a validation system before the first booking.

What I Would Test

01. Does the unified dashboard change behaviour: do users actually look at nutrition and training together? 02. Does contextual shopping convert: do goal-based suggestions outperform generic browsing? 03. Does the gym finder drive retention or is it a one-time feature? 04. Do users book expert sessions through the app or default to external channels?

The first experiment is smaller than the product vision.

The six-module V1 above describes the product direction. But the first product principle is "connect before expanding." Testing the entire ecosystem at once would make it impossible to isolate what works. The first experiment focuses on the smallest loop that proves the core hypothesis: does connected information create clearer fitness understanding?

Validation Plan

This is a proposed validation plan, not completed research.

Study Design

What I would
test first.

Participants: 5–7 people who currently use at least two fitness, nutrition, activity or wearable services. They should regularly review their fitness progress and represent a mix of structured and less structured routines. Recruitment would prioritise people already experiencing the fragmentation problem, not fitness novices.

Tasks

  • 01Set a fitness goal and complete initial personalisation.
  • 02Explain what the dashboard communicates about training and nutrition.
  • 03Find out why a specific recommendation appeared.
  • 04Change or revoke shared information.
  • 05Find relevant professional support nearby.
  • 06Assess whether a contextual shop recommendation feels helpful or intrusive.

What I Would Capture

Task completion rates. Dashboard comprehension accuracy. Misinterpretation of metrics. Recommendation trust level. Ability to find permission controls. Confidence in professional verification. Perceived value compared with the participant's current app stack. Moments of confusion or perceived overreach.

Each result would inform a specific product decision. Task 2 validates the dashboard hierarchy. Task 3 tests recommendation transparency. Task 6 determines whether contextual commerce creates value or suspicion.

Two rounds, two different questions.

Round 1 — Core Value

Purpose: Test whether the connected experience is understandable and useful.

Tasks: Complete goal-based onboarding · Interpret the unified dashboard · Explain why a recommendation appeared · Change or revoke a permission.

Signals: Task completion · Dashboard comprehension · Recommendation understanding · Permission-control findability · Perceived value versus current tools.

This would determine: Whether the connected-dashboard proposition is worth developing further.

Round 2 — Ecosystem Trust

Purpose: Test whether users trust the broader platform proposition.

Tasks: Find an appropriate professional · Understand professional verification · Review a contextual product recommendation · Distinguish organic and sponsored recommendations · Report unsuitable advice or content.

Signals: Professional trust · Commercial recommendation trust · Sponsored-content comprehension · Perceived product overreach · Reporting-flow findability.

This would determine: Whether expert access and commerce belong inside the same product or should remain separate services.

Combining every question into one session would create fatigue and weak evidence. The first round tests the central proposition; the second tests whether the broader ecosystem earns trust.

Neither round has been conducted.

Proposed thresholds for the first study

These thresholds are decision criteria for a future study, not measured results.

  • 01At least 5 of 7 participants correctly explain the dashboard's primary insight without prompting.
  • 02At least 5 of 7 complete goal-based onboarding without assistance.
  • 03All participants can identify why a recommendation appeared.
  • 04Any failure to locate and revoke professional data access is treated as a critical issue requiring redesign.
  • 05At least 5 of 7 correctly distinguish an organic recommendation from sponsored content.
  • 06Any interpretation of the product as providing a medical diagnosis is treated as a critical comprehension failure.
  • 07At least 5 of 7 participants describe the unified view as more useful than manually comparing their current tools and can explain why.

These thresholds are directional decision criteria for an early qualitative study. They are not statistically representative product benchmarks.

Reflection

Whether fitness consumers want depth or breadth is the open question. Every successful fitness app has won by doing one thing well. Tips4Fit proposes that the connections between those things matter more than any single feature. The market has not validated that yet.

Worked: The dark UI creates visual separation from the clinical white look most health apps default to. Dashboard unification puts nutrition and training on equal footing. Keeping the shop inside the app rather than linking out avoids the "app within an app" problem.

Would change: Stronger social layer. More home screen states showing different goal types. The explore module needs a clearer hierarchy between trainers, doctors, and gyms.

Concept work. Not shipped. Not validated. The value is in the system design: how six modules share one profile, one data layer, and one visual language without becoming noise.
